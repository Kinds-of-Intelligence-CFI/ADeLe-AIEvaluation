# Communication and social interaction

This rubric assesses the difficulty level of tasks requiring an interaction to be steered. Steering covers choosing what to say so that another party comes to understand, accept or do what the task needs from them. It ranges from tasks that need nothing from anyone to those in which several parties in conflict must be brought along together.

What this dimension does not cover. This dimension concerns steering an interaction rather than composing its messages or understanding its parties. How well content must be expressed, in vocabulary, genre, register, structure and fit to audience, does not raise this demand. Inferring what others believe, intend or feel is understanding them rather than steering them, and raises this demand only insofar as what is inferred must shape what is said next. Establishing the state of the non-social environment is not communication and makes no demand here. Note, mastering one's own impulses during the exchange is not scored either, since it describes the solver rather than a demand the task makes. The stakes of the situation, the number of people addressed, and how hard someone who is refused keeps pushing do not by themselves raise the demand.

Level 0: None. The task does not require communication, or needs nothing from another party.
Examples:
* Prove that there are infinitely many prime numbers.
* Transcribe a recorded lecture into text.
* Find the cheapest combination of flights for a fixed itinerary.

Level 1: Very low. The task requires communication that a familiar script covers, or a message sent with no reply to adapt to. Nothing that is said depends on how the other party responds.
Examples:
* Order a coffee and a sandwich at a café counter.
* Ask a librarian which shelf holds a book you can name.
* Message a dental practice to move an appointment from Tuesday to Thursday.
* Write an 1,100-word newspaper column arguing for a national carbon tax.

Level 2: Low. The task requires a cooperative exchange to be guided. What to say next depends on what the other party has just said, but nothing they want stands against what the task needs from them.
Examples:
* Take a patient's history in a clinic, where each answer determines which symptom to ask about next.
* Interview a job candidate about a project on their CV, following up on what they actually say.
* Work out over a support chat which of six broadband plans suits a customer who is unsure how they use the internet at home.

Level 3: Intermediate. The task requires someone to be won over. They do not yet accept what the task needs from them, and each doubt or objection they raise sets the next move. However, they are open to being persuaded.
Examples:
* Tell a colleague that the section they wrote for a shared report has to be rewritten before submission, when they are proud of it but will hear you out.
* Tell a patient, as their doctor, that they have a serious illness, and bring them to accept a treatment they are at first reluctant to start, working through each concern as it is raised.
* Settle a dispute between two flatmates over the cleaning rota, where each arrived wanting a different split and both will accept whatever they come to see as fair.

Level 4: High. The task requires someone to be brought round against their will. It cannot succeed unless they accept or do something they are working against, so each move must answer their counter-moves as they push back.
Examples:
* Persuade a landlord to release you from a lease six months early, when they have no reason to agree and would rather keep collecting the rent.
* Bring a customer who is shouting for a refund the policy does not allow to accept the store credit it does offer.
* Talk a co-founder out of a launch date they have staked their reputation on and still believe in.
* Agree a price with a market trader who opens far above what you will pay and gives ground only when you start to walk away.

Level 5: Very high. The task requires several parties in conflict to be brought along together. What the task needs from each cannot all be had at once, and every move toward one is felt by the others. Critically, if what the task needs from them is compatible, the task belongs below, however many parties there are.
Examples:
* Negotiate a hostage release in which the captor, the police commander pressing to storm the building, and the hostages' families each demand a different course, and each of them reads what is conceded to the others. The tactical plan for the operation is settled and not yours to make.
* Chair the closing night of a treaty negotiation where three delegations have stated public red lines that cannot all be honoured.
* As mediator, settle the custody arrangement in a divorce where both parents want the child to live with them and each expects the other to exploit any concession.
