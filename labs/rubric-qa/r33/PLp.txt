# Planning
#! ADeLe v2 (agentic) — DRAFT for team review | version: v2-draft | source: Paolo_Pablo | revised: 2026-07-27 | validated: labs/rubric-qa rounds 1-21 (incl. solver oracle)

This rubric assesses the level of cognitive demands associated with planning: envisioning a desired outcome and devising a sequence of actions or strategies to achieve it, including setting instrumental goals, dividing the task into subtasks, sequencing actions, and allocating limited resources among them. It concerns what is required to find a workable plan, not to carry it out: execution length is assessed by Volume (VO), sustained execution — including noticing that a plan needs revision — by Action Control and Execution (PLe), reasoning about other agents' beliefs and intentions by Mind Modelling (MSm), and inferring hidden or changing features of the environment by Situational and Environmental Understanding (PLs); such features raise the planning demand only insofar as they make a workable plan harder to find. The level of cognitive demands progresses from tasks whose plan is given or retrieved ready-made to those for which no established procedure exists and a workable plan must be invented. The demand is set by the hardest plan-finding the task still requires from the point being scored, whether the plan is devised at the outset or re-devised when execution shows it failing.

Level 0: None. The task requires no planning: the plan is given — the task is completed by a single action or retrieval, or by following a sequence of steps that is explicitly provided, so there are no decisions about what to do or in what order. This includes a task scored partway through whose remaining work is a single forced action. Critically, this holds regardless of how long or laborious the execution is.
Examples:
* What year did Darwin publish "The Origin of Species": 1856, 1859, 1865 or 1866?
* Convert a temperature from Celsius to Fahrenheit. (A single computation; there is nothing to sequence or decide.)
* Finish a puzzle from a position in which only one legal continuation remains.
* Implement accurate real-time translation of a conversation between English and Spanish. (Hard, but the difficulty is linguistic; no plan needs to be found.)
* Execute a 200-step laboratory protocol exactly as written, in the given order. (The plan already exists; carrying it out is execution, not planning.)

Level 1: Very low. Tasks at this level require the plan only to be retrieved: a single standard, well-established routine covers the whole task directly, so what to do follows from the routine rather than having to be worked out. Critically, recognising what kind of task it is does not by itself put a task here — the routine must determine the actions, whether it fixes their order or leaves it free; where the order itself has to be worked out rather than following from the routine, the plan is being searched for rather than retrieved. No decomposition into subtasks is needed.
Examples:
* Answer a customer's question by finding the relevant section of the product manual and writing the reply. (One familiar routine runs from question to answer; the task is not divided into stages.)
* Draft a brief email summarising the key points from a meeting after reading the notes.
* Write a covering letter for a job application, given the advertisement and the CV it responds to. (One well-established routine covers it end to end; nothing has to be divided into subtasks.)

Level 2: Low. Tasks at this level require the plan to be assembled: no single routine covers the whole task, so it must be divided into a few well-defined subtasks — but each subtask is covered by a standard step, and the steps do not constrain one another, so a sensible choice at one step never needs to be revised because of a later one. Critically, a task remains at this level however many steps it involves, so long as the steps stay independent.
Examples:
* Plan a day-trip itinerary given preferences (museums, restaurants, location); identify opening hours and schedule visits.
* Build a personal webpage using a template: choose a template, customise the layout, and deploy the site.
* Plan a small data analysis: download the dataset, clean it, run a statistical analysis and produce a chart — a standard pipeline whose stages do not constrain each other.

Level 3: Intermediate. Tasks at this level require the plan to be searched for: established methods exist for tasks of this kind and the subtasks can be listed at the outset, but decisions interact, so an option that looks good locally can be wrong because of its consequences several steps later, and alternatives must be compared by looking ahead before committing. Recognising what kind of task it is already supplies the outline of a workable plan; the work is choosing and adapting among known options. Critically, this level can be reached by a static, fully observable, single-agent task.
Examples:
* Plan a multi-city international trip where flight dates, visa processing times and bookings constrain one another. (Contrast the day-trip at Level 2, whose choices are independent.)
* Find a strong plan in a club-level chess middlegame, where a natural-looking move can fail to a consequence several moves deep. (Nothing is hidden, and the opponent's replies are explored as branches of the same search rather than by modelling a mind.)
* Solve a contest programming problem in which several standard techniques could apply and the data structure settled on early decides whether the later queries can be answered in time, so the options must be compared before committing.

Level 4: High. Tasks at this level require the structure of the plan to be constructed: no established procedure or template supplies the decomposition into subtasks, so the decomposition itself must be discovered — the choices are strongly interdependent, and most approaches that appear viable fail for reasons that only become evident when explored. Critically, recognising what kind of task it is does not by itself yield a plan: a task-specific insight must be found before the subtasks can even be laid out.
Examples:
* Design the plan for replicating a machine-learning paper that omits key details: what to implement, in what order, and which checks would show whether a discrepancy is a bug or a difference in setup.
* Solve a contest programming problem for which no standard technique applies: the key idea must be found first, and most approaches that look promising fail only once they are worked through.
* Plan the first ascent of an unclimbed face for which no route description exists: which line to attempt, where camps can be placed at all, and what to carry all depend on one another, and candidate lines mostly turn out to be impassable only once they have been worked through.

Level 5: Very high. Tasks at this level require the plan to be invented: no established procedure exists for the task at all, the space of candidate plans is vast, and workable plans are so rare that finding one constitutes a novel discovery. The decomposition into subgoals must be created from scratch, with whole families of candidate approaches generated, evaluated and discarded. Critically, a task belongs here only where no established procedure exists for it at all: where the decomposition must be discovered but the kind of work is itself established, the task belongs at the level below.
Examples:
* Devise a synthesis route for a complex, previously unsynthesised natural product, where almost every sequence of reactions fails.
* Devise the proof strategy for a long-standing open conjecture, where the lemmas to aim for must themselves be discovered and restructured as attempts collapse.
* Plan a multi-year research programme against an unsolved scientific problem — which sub-questions to pose, which to abandon — where whole lines of attack die only once explored.
