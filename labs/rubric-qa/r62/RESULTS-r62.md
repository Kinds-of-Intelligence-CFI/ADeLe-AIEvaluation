## The clause works, and chess is not the case it reaches

T3 is the clean result: draughts moved to 2, and both stronger judges quoted the new clause
verbatim to get there. Opus applied it to chess too. Haiku and sonnet did not, and sonnet said
exactly why:

> the exclusion note that choosing a candidate requires simulating its consequences

That is the instrumental-scope clause: *"a candidate can rarely be chosen until its consequences
have been traced, and that tracing is simulating."* It is the clause Pablo installed deliberately
when he reversed the scope, and on a task phrased as **find a plan** it fires first and pulls the
item to 3.

So the two clauses pull against each other on precisely this item. The new one says the rules
supply the model; the older one says the tracing needed to choose a candidate is simulating. Chess
triggers both. Draughts, which asks for a fact about the position rather than for a plan, triggers
only the first, and lands where Pablo's reasoning says it should.

**This is a precedence question between two clauses, not a wording slip**, so it goes back to Pablo
rather than getting patched. The options are to say that the rules clause takes precedence when the
options are rule-governed, to accept that a task phrased as planning carries the higher score, or
to conclude that chess-the-plan really is a 3 and only chess-the-position is a 2.

## T4 was my prediction failing, not the text

I predicted 3 for the negotiation whose concessions shift the other side's threshold. All three
judges said 5, citing the reflexive feedback clause and comparing it to the arms-talks example.
They are right and the reading is consistent: beliefs about the other's threshold feed back and do
so again. **Ninth prediction of mine to lose to the text.** It is also a useful data point, since
it shows the Level 5 key firing on a small two-party situation rather than only on large social ones.
