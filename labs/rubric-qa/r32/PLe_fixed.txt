# Action control and execution
#! ADeLe v2 (agentic) — DRAFT for team review | version: v2-draft | source: Paolo_Pablo | revised: 2026-07-27 | validated: labs/rubric-qa rounds 5-21 (incl. rivercross)

This rubric assesses the level of cognitive demands associated with action control and execution: carrying out a plan and keeping it on track — monitoring progress, deciding what to advance next and when a part is done, detecting deviations (a step done wrong, or a plan no longer working), and recovering at the right grain, from redoing a step to revising the plan. Noticing that revision is needed is assessed here; devising it is Planning (PLp), execution length Volume (VO), other agents Mind Modelling (MSm), hidden or changing environment state Situational and Environmental Understanding (PLs), and holding interim results the memory dimensions (MMs, MMe) — such features raise this demand only insofar as they make deviations harder to detect or recover from. An action is the carrying-out of one plan step: the finest unit that could be separately instructed, done wrong, and redone (posting an entry, making a tool call, writing a section); words and tokens are not actions, and the reasoning that finds or chooses a step is deliberation, assessed by Planning. Feedback is information from the environment that establishes, without further work, that an action or subtask has succeeded — not an observation from which success must still be worked out, nor an interlocutor's acknowledgement. The level of cognitive demands progresses from tasks in which the environment checks every step to those in which errors cannot be undone and must be prevented before each commitment. Where a task shows features of more than one level, the demand is set by the highest it genuinely requires.

Level 0: None. The task requires no execution control: it is completed by a single atomic action — no monitoring, sequencing or recovery is involved, and the action succeeds or fails at once. An answer that deliberation can produce whole is executed by mere transcription, however hard it was to find, and belongs here. Critically, a single action that cannot be undone does not belong here however simple it is to perform: what places a task here is that a mistake costs nothing but doing it again.
Examples:
* Say whether checkmate can be delivered in one move in a given chess position.
* Look up a single fact with one search-engine query.
* Select the figure that fits the pattern from A to E.
* Find the winning line in a given chess position and write it down. (All the difficulty is in finding the plan; carrying it out is a single act of transcription.)

Level 1: Very low. Tasks at this level require execution to be checked by the environment at every step: feedback arrives after essentially every action — each action's success or failure is signalled immediately — so an error is caught and corrected on the spot and no self-monitoring is needed. An environment that refuses invalid actions belongs here only where being accepted establishes that the step did what it was for; where an action can be accepted and still be useless — a legal move that makes no progress — that refusal is not feedback and the task belongs higher. An environment that continuously displays its state is not for that reason checking the agent's steps: where the display shows what is happening but whether an action achieved its purpose must still be read off and interpreted, that is an observation and not feedback, and the task belongs higher. Critically, a task remains at this level however many steps it involves, so long as every step is immediately checked.
Examples:
* Reset a password by following the prompts on a website.
* Navigate to an address following turn-by-turn directions, where any wrong turn is immediately flagged and rerouted.
* Cook from a recipe in which each step's outcome makes success immediately apparent before moving on.

Level 2: Low. Tasks at this level require execution to be checked at checkpoints: feedback arrives roughly once every two to ten actions at natural checkpoints such as the completion of a subtask, or at the end of a short task. Between checkpoints the agent must keep track of what has been done and verify its own steps, but an error discovered at a checkpoint is cheap to localise and redo. Critically, what the subtasks are is given or obvious; deciding the decomposition belongs to Planning.
Examples:
* Assemble a flat-pack wardrobe, where nothing reveals whether a stage was done right until a whole sub-assembly is stood up and either fits together or does not.
* Follow a tutorial to implement a small transformer network, running the code at each milestone.
* Complete a long structured application form that validates each section on submission.

Level 3: Intermediate. Tasks at this level require execution to be self-checked: stretches of tens of actions — or the whole task — pass without any feedback, so the agent must set its own criteria for being on track, monitor progress against them, and decide when each part is finished, which open subtask to advance next, and whether the current approach is still on course. A deliverable whose earlier parts are committed and then used by later ones is executed through as many actions as it has separately redoable steps, so it belongs at least here however it is finally handed over; holding those interim parts in mind is assessed by the memory dimensions, not here. Critically, a task remains at this level as long as an error, once noticed, can be fixed where it occurred without redoing the work that followed.
Examples:
* Write and polish the introduction of a scientific paper, judging for yourself when each paragraph is finished.
* Summarise a long document and extract its main arguments. (A weak paragraph is fixed in place; nothing downstream depends on it.)
* Draft the claims of a patent from the paper it is based on, with no examiner feedback until the whole set is filed.

Level 4: High. Tasks at this level require execution to be safeguarded against propagation: feedback is equally sparse, but an undetected error silently corrupts the work that follows, so monitoring must be proactive — the agent must anticipate where slips would matter, insert its own checks, and, when an error is found, trace its consequences and rework everything that depends on it — or recognise that the approach itself must change before more work is built on it. Taking the wrong branch of a conditional plan belongs here when the mistake is carried forward unnoticed, and lower when the next observation reveals it.
Examples:
* Manually reconcile a year of a small business's ledger across twelve monthly statements, where a misposted entry in an early month silently unbalances every subsequent account until the final totals are compared. (Contrast the summary at Level 3: there an error is fixed in place; here it forces rework of every month that followed.)
* Extend a large codebase that has no test suite, where a subtle early bug only surfaces much later in dependent components.
* Translate a long legal contract, where a mistranslated defined term early on corrupts every later clause that uses it.

Level 5: Very high. Tasks at this level require execution to be safeguarded before every commitment: some actions are irreversible, so errors cannot be corrected even when found — they must be prevented, and each consequential step verified before it is taken. A consequential irreversible step places a task here on its own, whether or not feedback is sparse and whether or not errors propagate: an action confirmed the instant it is taken is still unrecoverable if it cannot be undone. Time pressure raises the demand only by forcing commitments before they can be verified: spending one of a strictly limited number of attempts is such an action, as is committing because a deadline leaves no time to check; a deadline that still allows each step to be verified does not put a task here.
Examples:
* Migrate a live production database without a rollback mechanism, where a wrong step corrupts data irrecoverably.
* Carry out a multi-step synthesis of a scarce compound, where a failed step destroys the material. (Contrast Planning, which assesses devising the route: here the route is known and the demand is that no step can be retried.)
* Conduct an archaeological excavation, where each removal destroys context that can never be re-examined.
