# Visual processing
#! ADeLe v2 (agentic) — DRAFT for team review | version: v2-draft | source: Paolo_Pablo | rewritten: 2026-07-29 | driver: distance from direct readout (docs/sensory-rubric-plan.md §2)

This rubric assesses the level of cognitive demands associated with visual processing: recovering the information a task needs from a visual signal, in cases where the signal does not simply present it. The demand is set by how far the required information sits from a direct readout — shown plainly, interleaved with a background, partly destroyed, spread across views none of which suffices, or reachable only by reasoning backwards through how the image was formed. The level of cognitive demands progresses from tasks carrying no visual signal at all to those in which the content is not present in the image in any form and can be recovered only from a model of how the image came about. Where a task shows features of more than one level, the demand is set by the highest it genuinely requires.

Distinguishing visual processing from neighbouring dimensions. This dimension scores getting the content out of the signal, not what is done with it afterwards. Locating a target among distractors is assessed by Attention and Scan (AS), which owns clutter-search; what the recovered content means is assessed by the knowledge dimensions, by Conceptualisation, Learning and Abstraction (CL) and by Mind Modelling (MS); rotating, projecting or otherwise transforming a shape that is already legible is Spatio-physical Reasoning (SNs); and how long perception must be sustained is Volume (VO). Producing images is not perception and is scored nowhere here. A scene containing a great many elements, all of them clear, carries no demand on this dimension.

Level 0: None. The task carries no visual signal, or any image it carries is incidental and nothing the task needs is read from it.
Examples:
* Answer a question about a passage of supplied text.
* Compute a total from figures given in words.
* Summarise a telephone conversation supplied as a written transcript.

Level 1: Very low. Tasks at this level require the information to be read off as presented: it is in focus, unobstructed and at ordinary scale, and no part of it has to be recovered. Critically, a busy image is still at this level so long as what the task needs is itself plainly shown — visual complexity is not perceptual demand, and searching a cluttered scene for a target is Attention and Scan (AS).
Examples:
* Read the time from a clock face photographed straight on in good light.
* State the colour of a car that fills most of a clear photograph.
* Read the number on a road sign photographed from directly in front of it in daylight.

Level 2: Low. Tasks at this level require the information to be separated: it is present and intact, but interleaved with a background that does not resemble it, so it must be isolated before it can be read. Critically, what marks this level is that something must be pulled apart, not that there is a lot of it. Where the target is already distinct and the only work is finding it, the demand is Attention and Scan (AS). The test is overlay against absence: at this level the whole of the target is still there, laid over or under something else; where part of it is missing — hidden behind an object, or destroyed — the task is reconstruction and belongs higher.
Examples:
* Read a line of handwriting written across a printed form, where the ink crosses the ruled boxes.
* Read a subtitle rendered in white text over a pale, shifting background.
* Transcribe a receipt photographed on a patterned tablecloth whose pattern shows through the paper. Where the receipt lies in the frame is given.

Level 3: Intermediate. Tasks at this level require the information to be reconstructed: part of it is destroyed, occluded or masked, so what survives must be completed using regularities of the domain — how objects, surfaces or type usually behave. Critically, one view is at this level however badly degraded it is; a single view that still contains enough to complete the content does not belong higher.
Examples:
* Read a water-damaged page on which roughly a third of the characters are illegible and must be inferred from the words around them.
* Identify a vehicle from one photograph in which a pillar hides most of its body.
* Read a licence plate from a single frame blurred by motion.

Level 4: High. Tasks at this level require the information to be combined: no single view carries it, and several individually insufficient views must be put together across space, time or modality, any one of them alone leaving the content undetermined. Critically, being given several views does not by itself place a task here — what marks this level is that no one of them suffices.
Examples:
* Determine the shape of an object from a set of photographs, each showing only a partial silhouette against a bright background. Which object it is, once the shape is known, is not in question.
* Read a document photographed in overlapping strips, each cut mid-line, where the lines must be rejoined across strips before anything can be read.
* Establish which of two near-identical rooms a photograph was taken in, where the answer follows only from details that appear in different frames of a walkthrough and in none of them together.

Level 5: Very high. Tasks at this level require the information to be inverted: no direct route to it exists, and recovery depends on knowing how the signal was formed and working backwards through that formation — the optics, the sensor, the encoding or the degradation itself. Critically, severity is not what places a task here; a very badly corrupted single image is still reconstruction. What marks this level is that the content is not present in the image in any form.
Examples:
* Recover the surface relief of an object from the way light falls across it in a single photograph.
* Determine what was written on a missing page from the impressions it left on the sheet beneath.
* Establish the layout of a room from a photograph of its reflection in a curved surface.
