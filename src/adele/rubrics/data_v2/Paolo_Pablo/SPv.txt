# Visual processing
#! ADeLe v2 (agentic) — DRAFT for team review | version: v2-draft | source: Paolo_Pablo | revised: 2026-08-16 | driver: how far the needed information sits from a direct readout of the signal | differentiation section made catalogue-independent (no named sibling dimensions)

This rubric assesses the level of cognitive demands associated with visual processing: recovering the information a task needs from a visual signal, in cases where the signal does not simply present it. The demand is set by one question: how much of what the task needs is carried by the image itself, and how much must be supplied from beyond it? The scale runs from tasks that carry no visual signal, through images that present the content plainly, hold it under interference, or carry only part of it, to images that do not carry the content at all and yield it only by reasoning back through how the image was formed.

What this dimension does not cover. This dimension scores getting the content out of the signal, not what is done with it afterwards: what the recovered content means, what it implies, or what knowledge interpreting it takes does not raise this demand. Nor is the demand raised by how hard a target is to locate among distractors — search is not extraction, and a target merely hard to find but plainly legible once found makes no demand here — nor by rotating, projecting or otherwise transforming a shape that is already legible, nor by how long perception must be sustained. Producing images is generation, not perception, and is not scored here. A scene of many elements, all of them clear, carries no demand on this dimension.

A word on what carried by the signal means. Information is carried when it is physically present in the image — there in the signal to be recovered — however fine, faint or small it is, and however much magnification or spectral reach drawing it out would take. Fineness alone does not raise the demand: a pattern that is present but hard to resolve stays at the level its recovery sets, since the content is in the image and only has to be brought out. An image may hold several registered layers or channels captured together: these are one image, and what they jointly hold is carried by it. What the image does not physically hold is not carried; content of that kind reaches the top level only where it can still be recovered by working back through how the image was formed. This keeps the scale a property of the image and the task, not of the acuity brought to reading it.

Level 0: None. The task carries no visual signal, or any image it carries is incidental and nothing the task needs is read from it.
Examples:
* Answer a question about a passage of supplied text.
* Compute a total from figures given in words.
* Summarise a telephone conversation supplied as a written transcript.

Level 1: Very low. Tasks at this level require the information to be read off as presented: the image carries it in full, in focus and at ordinary scale, and nothing has to be recovered. A crowded image stays here so long as what the task needs is itself plainly shown; the amount of other detail present is not itself perceptual demand.
Examples:
* Read the time from a clock face photographed straight on in good light.
* State the colour of a car that fills most of a clear photograph.
* Pick out which of ten sharply photographed coins is the largest, the answer plain in the single image.

Level 2: Low. Tasks at this level require the information to be separated: the whole of it is carried, but interleaved with a background that does not resemble it, so it must be isolated before it can be read. What raises the demand is that something must be pulled apart, not that there is a lot of it, and not that the target is merely hard to locate. The whole of the target is still there, laid over or under something else — where part of it is missing, the task is reconstruction and belongs higher.
Examples:
* Read a line of handwriting written across a printed form, where the ink crosses the ruled boxes.
* Read a subtitle rendered in white text over a pale, shifting background.
* Transcribe a receipt photographed on a patterned tablecloth whose pattern shows through the paper, where the receipt lies in the frame is given.

Level 3: Intermediate. Tasks at this level require the information to be reconstructed: the image carries only part of the content — the rest is destroyed, occluded or masked — so what survives must be completed from the regularities of the domain, how objects, surfaces or type usually behave. However badly the image is degraded, the task stays at this level so long as what survives carries enough to complete the content from within.
Examples:
* Read a water-damaged page on which roughly a third of the characters are illegible and must be inferred from the words around them.
* Identify a vehicle from one photograph in which a pillar hides most of its body.
* Read a licence plate from a single frame blurred by motion.

Level 4: High. Tasks at this level require the information to be combined: no single image carries it, so several — captured separately, from different viewpoints, times or channels, their relation to one another not given — must first be brought into correspondence, each alone leaving the content undetermined. What raises the demand is that the correspondence between the images must itself be established before the content exists at all; where they already stand in a fixed, known relation, they are one image and the task belongs below.
Examples:
* Determine the shape of an object from a set of photographs, each showing only a partial silhouette against a bright background. Which object it is, once the shape is known, is not in question.
* Read a document photographed in overlapping strips, each cut mid-line, where the lines must be rejoined across strips before anything can be read.
* Establish which of two near-identical rooms a photograph was taken in, where the answer follows only from details that appear in different frames of a walkthrough and in none of them together.

Level 5: Very high. Tasks at this level require the information to be inverted: the image does not carry the content as content at all, and recovery depends on modelling how the image was formed and working back through it — the optics, the capture, the encoding or the degradation itself. The content cannot be read off the image directly at all, because it is not present there; only the trace of the image's formation is, and the content follows only by inverting that formation.
Examples:
* Recover the surface relief of an object from the way light falls across it in a single photograph.
* Determine what was written on a missing page from the impressions it left on the sheet beneath.
* Establish the layout of a room from a photograph of its reflection in a curved surface.
