# Planning
#! ADeLe v2 (agentic) — DRAFT, unvalidated | version: v2-draft | source: Paolo_Pablo | revised: 2026-07-25 single-driver redesign

This criterion assesses the level of cognitive demands associated with planning: envisioning a desired outcome and devising a sequence of actions or strategies to achieve it, including setting instrumental goals, dividing the task into subtasks, sequencing actions, and allocating limited resources among them. It concerns what is required to find a workable plan, not to carry it out: execution length is assessed by Volume (VO), sustained execution — including noticing that a plan needs revision — by Action Control and Execution (PLe), reasoning about other agents' beliefs and intentions by Mind Modelling (MS), and inferring hidden or changing features of the environment by Environmental and Situational Understanding (MSe); such features raise the planning demand only insofar as they make a workable plan harder to find. The demand is set by the hardest plan-finding the task requires, whether devised at the outset or re-devised when execution shows the plan failing. Demand is a property of the task, not of any particular solver's behaviour; when in doubt between two levels, assign the lower unless the higher level's requirement is clearly met.

Level 0: None. This demand level is characterized by the plan being given: the task is completed by a single action or retrieval, or by following a sequence of steps that is explicitly provided, so there are no decisions about what to do or in what order. Critically, this holds regardless of how long or laborious the execution is.
Examples:
* What year did Darwin publish "The Origin of Species": 1856, 1859, 1865 or 1866?
* Convert a temperature from Celsius to Fahrenheit.
* Implement accurate real-time translation of a conversation between English and Spanish.
* Execute a 200-step laboratory protocol exactly as written, in the given order. (The plan already exists; carrying it out is execution, not planning.)

Level 1: Very low. This demand level is characterized by the plan being retrieved: a single standard, well-established routine covers the whole task directly, and where choices of order exist, any reasonable order succeeds. Critically, no decomposition into subtasks is needed.
Examples:
* Combine information from two sources to answer a simple question (e.g., compare the battery life of two smartphone models).
* Draft a brief email summarising the key points from a meeting after reading the notes.
* Decide the order in which to run three independent errands across town.

Level 2: Low. This demand level is characterized by the plan being assembled: no single routine covers the whole task, so it must be divided into a few well-defined subtasks — but each subtask is covered by a standard step, and the steps do not constrain one another, so a sensible choice at one step never needs to be revised because of a later one. Critically, a task remains at this level however many steps it involves, so long as the steps stay independent.
Examples:
* Plan a day-trip itinerary given preferences (museums, restaurants, location); identify opening hours and schedule visits.
* Build a personal webpage using a template: choose a template, customise the layout, and deploy the site.
* Plan a small data analysis: download the dataset, clean it, run a statistical analysis and produce a chart — a standard pipeline whose stages do not constrain each other.

Level 3: Intermediate. This demand level is characterized by the plan being searched for: established methods exist for tasks of this kind and the subtasks can be listed at the outset, but decisions interact, so an option that looks good locally can be wrong because of its consequences several steps later, and alternatives must be compared by looking ahead before committing. Recognizing what kind of task it is already supplies the outline of a workable plan; the work is choosing and adapting among known options. Critically, this level can be reached by a static, fully observable, single-agent task.
Examples:
* Plan a multi-city international trip where flight dates, visa processing times and bookings constrain one another. (Contrast the day-trip at Level 2, whose choices are independent.)
* Find a strong plan in a club-level chess middlegame, where a natural-looking move can fail to a consequence several moves deep. (Nothing is hidden, and the opponent's replies are explored as branches of the same search rather than by modelling a mind.)
* Solve a competition-programming problem of medium contest difficulty, where recognizing the type of problem points to the standard techniques that solve it.

Level 4: High. This demand level is characterized by the structure of the plan having to be constructed: no established procedure or template supplies the decomposition into subtasks, so the decomposition itself must be discovered — the choices are strongly interdependent, and most approaches that appear viable fail for reasons that only become evident when explored. Critically, recognizing what kind of task it is does not by itself yield a plan: a task-specific insight must be found before the subtasks can even be laid out.
Examples:
* Design the complete plan for replicating a complex research paper — what to implement, in what order, and how each stage is validated — where the right decomposition must be discovered rather than copied.
* Solve a competition-programming problem at the hardest contest tier, where knowing the standard toolkit does not by itself yield a plan and finding any correct approach is the principal difficulty.
* Plan a mountaineering expedition on a difficult unfamiliar route, where acclimatisation, weather windows, logistics and fallback options must be jointly worked out.

Level 5: Very high. This demand level is characterized by the plan being invented: no established procedure exists for the task at all, the space of candidate plans is vast, and workable plans are so rare that finding one constitutes a novel discovery. The decomposition into subgoals must be created from scratch, with whole families of candidate approaches generated, evaluated and discarded.
Examples:
* Devise a synthesis route for a complex, previously unsynthesised natural product, where almost every sequence of reactions fails.
* Devise the proof strategy for a hard open research problem, where the division into lemmas must itself be discovered.
* Plan a research agenda for attacking an unsolved scientific problem, where the division into sub-questions must itself be discovered.
