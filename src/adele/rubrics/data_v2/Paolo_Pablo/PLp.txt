This rubric assesses the difficulty level of tasks requiring a workable plan to be found. Planning covers envisioning a desired outcome and devising a sequence of actions to achieve it. It includes setting instrumental goals, dividing the task into subtasks, sequencing actions, and allocating limited resources among them. The difficulty ranges from tasks whose plan is given or retrieved ready-made to those for which no established procedure exists and a plan must be invented.

What this dimension does not cover. This dimension concerns what is required to find a workable plan rather than to carry it out. Sustained execution, keeping the work on track, and noticing along the way that a plan needs revision do not raise this demand, though devising the revision does. Nor is the demand raised by how long or laborious the execution is, nor by how many agents are involved or how their beliefs and intentions must be worked out. The same holds for how much must be found out about a hidden or changing environment, and for how open-ended the goal is. Note, such features raise this demand only insofar as they make a workable plan harder to find.

Level 0: None. The task requires no planning, because the plan is given. It is completed by a single action or retrieval, or by following a sequence of steps that is explicitly provided, so there are no decisions about what to do or in what order. This includes a task scored partway through whose remaining work is a single forced action. Critically, this holds regardless of how long or laborious the execution is.
Examples:
* What year did Darwin publish "The Origin of Species": 1856, 1859, 1865 or 1866?
* Convert 38 degrees Celsius to Fahrenheit.
* Finish a puzzle from a position in which only one legal continuation remains.
* Implement accurate real-time translation of a conversation between English and Spanish.
* Execute a 200-step laboratory protocol exactly as written, in the given order.

Level 1: Very low. The task requires the plan only to be retrieved. A single standard, well-established routine covers the whole task directly, so what to do follows from the routine rather than having to be worked out. Importantly, recognising what kind of task it is does not by itself put a task here, since the routine must determine the actions, whether it fixes their order or leaves it free. If the order itself has to be worked out rather than following from the routine, the plan is being searched for rather than retrieved. No decomposition into subtasks is needed.
Examples:
* Answer a customer's question by finding the relevant section of a 60-page product manual and writing the reply.
* Draft a brief email summarising the key points from a meeting after reading the notes.
* Write a covering letter for a job application, given the advertisement and the CV it responds to.

Level 2: Low. The task requires the plan to be assembled. No single routine covers the whole of it, so it must be divided into a few well-defined subtasks. Each subtask is covered by a standard step, and the steps do not constrain one another, so a sensible choice at one step never needs to be revised because of a later one. Critically, the task remains at this level however many steps it involves, so long as the steps stay independent.
Examples:
* Plan one day in a city from stated preferences for three museums and two restaurants, given their opening hours.
* Build a personal webpage using a template: choose a template, customise the layout, and deploy the site.
* Plan a small data analysis by downloading a 40,000-row dataset, cleaning it, running a statistical analysis and producing a chart. The stages of this standard pipeline do not constrain each other.

Level 3: Intermediate. The task requires the plan to be searched for. Established methods exist for tasks of this kind and the subtasks can be listed at the outset, but the decisions interact. An option that looks good locally can be wrong because of its consequences several steps later, so alternatives must be compared by looking ahead before committing. Recognising what kind of task it is already supplies the outline of a workable plan, and the work is choosing and adapting among known options. Note, a static, fully observable, single-agent task can reach this level.
Examples:
* Plan a trip through four cities where two require visas that take up to three weeks, and a conference on the ninth day cannot be moved. Nothing is reserved, and the deliverable is the itinerary.
* Find a strong plan in a club-level chess middlegame, where a natural-looking move can fail to a consequence four moves deep.
* Solve a contest programming problem in which several standard techniques could apply. The data structure settled on early decides whether the later queries can be answered in time, so the options must be compared before committing.

Level 4: High. The task requires the structure of the plan to be constructed. No established procedure or template supplies the decomposition into subtasks, so the decomposition itself must be discovered. The choices are strongly interdependent, and most approaches that appear viable fail for reasons that only become evident when explored. Importantly, recognising what kind of task it is does not by itself yield a plan, since a task-specific insight must be found before the subtasks can even be laid out.
Examples:
* Design the plan for replicating a machine-learning paper that omits key details, covering what to implement, in what order, and which checks would show whether a discrepancy is a bug or a difference in setup.
* Solve a contest programming problem for which no standard technique applies. The key idea must be found first, and most approaches that look promising fail only once they are worked through.
* Plan the first ascent of an unclimbed face for which no route description exists. Which line to attempt, where camps can be placed at all, and what to carry all depend on one another, and candidate lines mostly turn out to be impassable only once they have been worked through. The deliverable is the route plan, and the climb is not attempted here.

Level 5: Very high. The task requires the plan to be invented. No established procedure exists for it at all, the space of candidate plans is vast, and workable plans are so rare that finding one constitutes a novel discovery. The decomposition into subgoals must be created from scratch, with whole families of candidate approaches generated, evaluated and discarded. Note, a task belongs here only where no established procedure exists for it at all, and where the decomposition must be discovered but the kind of work is itself established, the task belongs at the level below.
Examples:
* Devise a synthesis route for a complex, previously unsynthesised natural product, where almost every sequence of reactions fails. The route is designed on paper, and nothing is run at the bench.
* Devise the proof strategy for a long-standing open conjecture, where the lemmas to aim for must themselves be discovered and restructured as attempts collapse. Writing the finished argument out is not the difficulty.
* Plan a multi-year research programme against an unsolved scientific problem, deciding which sub-questions to pose and which to abandon, where whole lines of attack die only once explored. The programme is written as a proposal rather than carried out.
