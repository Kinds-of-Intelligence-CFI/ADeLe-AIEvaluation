# Action control and execution
#! ADeLe v2 (agentic) — DRAFT, unvalidated | version: v2-draft | source: Paolo_Pablo | revised: 2026-07-25 single-driver redesign

This criterion assesses the level of cognitive demands associated with action control and execution: carrying out a plan and keeping the pursuit of the goal on track — monitoring progress, deciding what to advance next and when a part is done, detecting deviations (a step done wrong, or a plan no longer working), and recovering at the right grain, from redoing a step to triggering a revision of the plan. Noticing that revision is needed is assessed here; devising the plan or its revision is assessed by Planning (PLp), execution length by Volume (VO), reasoning about other agents by Mind Modelling (MS), hidden or changing environment state by Situational and Environmental Understanding (PLs), and holding interim results in mind by the memory dimensions — such features raise this demand only insofar as they make deviations harder to detect or to recover from. An action is the carrying-out of one plan step: the finest unit of execution that could be separately instructed, done wrong, and redone (posting an entry, making a tool call, writing a section); words and tokens are not actions, and the reasoning that finds or chooses a step is deliberation, assessed by Planning. Feedback is information from the environment that establishes, without further work, that an action or subtask has succeeded — the task's success signal, not mere observation of the new state nor an interlocutor's acknowledgement — and it is sparse when many actions pass before it arrives. An answer that deliberation can produce whole is executed by mere transcription, however hard it was to find; a deliverable whose earlier parts are committed and then used by later ones is executed through as many actions as it has separately redoable steps, even if handed over all at once. Time pressure raises the demand only insofar as it forces commitments before they can be verified; spending from a limited budget (attempts, materials, money) is an irreversible commitment; and in a conditional plan, selecting at each branch point the step matching the observed outcome is execution — a wrong selection typically surfaces late — while recognising a hidden situation is assessed by PLs.

Level 0: None. This demand level is characterized by execution being a single atomic action: no monitoring, sequencing or recovery is involved, and the action succeeds or fails at once.
Examples:
* Evaluate the possibility of achieving checkmate in chess in a single move.
* Do a Google search from google.com.
* Select the figure that fits the pattern from A to E.

Level 1: Very low. This demand level is characterized by execution being checked by the environment at every step: feedback arrives after essentially every action — each action's success or failure is signalled immediately, or invalid actions are simply not accepted — so an error is caught and corrected on the spot and no self-monitoring is needed. Critically, a task remains at this level however many steps it involves, so long as every step is immediately checked.
Examples:
* Reset a password by following the prompts on a website.
* Navigate to an address following turn-by-turn directions, where any wrong turn is immediately flagged and rerouted.
* Cook from a recipe in which each step's outcome makes success immediately apparent before moving on.
* Find the winning line in a given chess position and write it down. (All the difficulty is in finding the plan; carrying it out is a single step.)

Level 2: Low. This demand level is characterized by execution being checked at checkpoints: feedback arrives roughly once every two to ten actions, at natural checkpoints such as the completion of a subtask, or at the end of a short task. Between checkpoints the agent must keep track of what has been done and verify its own steps, but an error discovered at a checkpoint is cheap to localise and redo. Critically, what the subtasks are is given or obvious; deciding the decomposition belongs to Planning.
Examples:
* Build a piece of furniture from instructions, where each completed stage visibly fits or does not.
* Follow a tutorial to implement a small transformer network, running the code at each milestone.
* Complete a long structured application form that validates each section on submission.

Level 3: Intermediate. This demand level is characterized by execution being self-checked: stretches of tens of actions — or the whole task — pass without any feedback, so the agent must set its own criteria for being on track, monitor progress against them, and decide when each part is finished, which open subtask to advance next, and whether the current approach is still on course. Critically, a task remains at this level as long as an error, once noticed, can be fixed where it occurred without redoing the work that followed.
Examples:
* Write and polish the introduction of a scientific paper, judging for yourself when each paragraph is finished.
* Summarise a long document and extract its main arguments. (A weak paragraph is fixed in place; nothing downstream depends on it.)
* Draft the claims of a patent from the paper it is based on, with no examiner feedback until the whole set is filed.

Level 4: High. This demand level is characterized by execution having to be safeguarded against propagation: feedback is equally sparse, but an undetected error silently corrupts the work that follows, so monitoring must be proactive — the agent must anticipate where slips would matter, insert its own checks, and, when an error is found, trace its consequences and rework everything that depends on it — or recognise that the approach itself must change before more work is built on it.
Examples:
* Manually reconcile a year of a small business's ledger across twelve monthly statements, where a misposted entry in an early month silently unbalances every subsequent account until the final totals are compared. (Contrast the summary at Level 3: there an error is fixed in place; here it forces rework of every month that followed.)
* Extend a large codebase that has no test suite, where a subtle early bug only surfaces much later in dependent components.
* Translate a long legal contract, where a mistranslated defined term early on corrupts every later clause that uses it.

Level 5: Very high. This demand level is characterized by execution having to be safeguarded before every commitment: in addition to sparse feedback and silent propagation, some actions are irreversible, so errors cannot be corrected even when found — they must be prevented, and each consequential step verified before it is taken.
Examples:
* Migrate a live production database without a rollback mechanism, where a wrong step corrupts data irrecoverably.
* Carry out a multi-step synthesis of a scarce compound, where a failed step destroys the material.
* Conduct an archaeological excavation, where each removal destroys context that can never be re-examined.
