This rubric assesses the level of cognitive demands associated with simulating: working out how a situation will unfold. The situation is given. What is asked is what happens next, or what happens in the end. The situation may be physical, mechanical, biological, social or economic. What is simulated is what the world does. What to do about it is not scored here. Demand rises with how much change must be carried forward, and how finely. It falls when a model carries the change instead. A rule that covers the whole course — a law, a stated dynamic, a familiar regularity — does the carrying for you: the answer is computed, not tracked. Where no rule covers the course, the detail must be carried step by step.

What this dimension does not cover. Choosing among your own actions is planning. A candidate action enters here only as a stipulated change whose consequences must be traced. Knowing the governing regularity is knowledge, and is not scored here. Finding out the current state is perception or search; propagation is scored from the situation as given. Arithmetic is not simulating: computing an exact value along an undeflected course does not raise this demand. Neither does inner churn, where the answer survives a rough account of it. Other agents enter as processes, and their described tendencies are propagated like any other. Working out what they believe or want does not raise this demand.

Level 0: None. Nothing has to be run forward. The situation is static, or its outcome is already stated, or the question is about the state as given. A situation named but not presented also belongs here: with nothing given, there is nothing to run.
Examples:
* Given the full record of a finished match, say which side won.
* Say whether a completed Sudoku grid is valid.
* Given the guest list and the seating rules, say whether the seating chart breaks any of them.

Level 1: Very low. One step. A familiar process advances once. Nothing deflects it. The outcome follows at once, and a rough projection lands on it.
Examples:
* A glass is knocked off a table onto a stone floor. Say what happens to it.
* A match is touched to dry straw. Say what happens.
* A driver is described as short-tempered. Another car cuts in front of them. Say how they react.

Level 2: Low. A rule carries the course. One process runs on, or several run on without touching each other. A known regularity covers the whole way, so the answer is computed or projected roughly. The horizon does not matter. Neither does the exactness of the answer.
Examples:
* A tap runs into a bath with the plug in. Say whether it overflows before morning.
* Ice sits in a drink left in full sun. Say whether it has melted by evening.
* A ball is thrown at a given speed and angle. Ignoring air, compute exactly where it lands.

Level 3: Intermediate. Processes touch. A few influence one another, and the interaction changes the answer. Projecting each alone gives the wrong result, so the coupling must be carried together. The answer stays coarse. Once the coupling's effect is caught, a loose account still lands on it.
Examples:
* A lane closes on a busy road. Drivers move to the side streets, which fill and send some of them back. Say whether journeys across the area get longer.
* Foxes arrive on a hillside of rabbits. More rabbits feed more foxes; more foxes thin the rabbits. Say whether the rabbits keep increasing.
* A shop raises its prices. Customers drift to the rival across the square, whose queues lengthen. Say whether the shop takes more money over the month.

Level 4: High. The answer is fine. The interaction must be carried faithfully all the way. Drop one coupling and the result flips; track one loosely and it flips too. No single process settles the answer by its rough course. Few couplings or many, what places a task here is the fineness the answer demands.
Examples:
* A drought year on a smallholding. The crops share one irrigation channel, each harbours pests that reach the others, and the planting dates are staggered. Say which crops fail.
* A machine of tilting trays, rolling balls and counterweights is set going. Say whether the bell at the end rings.
* A predator is removed from a described food web. Say which populations rise and which crash.

Level 5: Very high. Courses diverge. Small differences early grow as the change plays out, so nearby starts end far apart. The task asks which course the situation actually takes. No rule compresses the run, so the fineness of the level below must be held the whole way. Divergence alone does not place a task here. Where a coarse answer would survive it, the task sits where its own question puts it.
Examples:
* From a described break, say which pocket the ball nearest the cushion reaches.
* From described conditions, say which of two neighbouring valleys the storm strikes three days out.
* A run starts on one bank. Each depositor's move shifts the odds for the next, and the town sits between the two outcomes. Say whether it spreads to the second bank.
