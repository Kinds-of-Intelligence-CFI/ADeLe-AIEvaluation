This rubric assesses the difficulty level of tasks requiring a situation to be modelled and run forward. The situation may be physical, mechanical, biological, social or economic, and what is asked is what it does next. The difficulty rises with the complexity of the model needed and with the accuracy it must have. It ranges from tasks needing no model at all to those needing a model of several interacting parts, run accurately enough to tell two similar courses apart. A rule that covers the whole course supplies the model ready-made. The answer is then computed rather than stepped through, and the difficulty stays low however long the horizon or exact the answer required. It also stays low wherever the solver can run the system and look, since the world then carries the change.

What this dimension does not cover. Nothing is scored here unless something in the situation changes over the course, so optimising or checking a fixed configuration makes no demand however hard it is. Searching among one's own candidate actions is planning, and the size of that search is not scored here. Critically, a candidate can rarely be chosen until its consequences have been traced, and that tracing is simulating, whether the task asks for the outcome directly or for the action that turns on it. Having the governing regularity is knowledge rather than simulating, so an obscure law does not raise this demand. What is scored is assembling this situation's model from regularities already held. Computing a value once the model yields a closed form is quantitative work. Finding out the current state is perception or search, and the model is built from the situation as given. Note, other agents enter as parts of the model whose described tendencies are run forward like any other, while working out what they believe or want belongs elsewhere.

Level 0: None. The task requires no model at all. Nothing in the situation changes over the course, because it is static, its outcome is already stated, or the question concerns the state as it is given. Note, a situation that the task names but does not present belongs here too, since there is nothing to build a model of.
Examples:
* The full move list of a finished chess game is given. Which side won?
* A completed Sudoku grid is given. Is it valid?
* A fixed string is given. What is the greatest value of (j minus i) times (k minus j) across triplets matching a stated pattern? (Nothing changes over a course, so the difficulty is combinatorial rather than simulating.)
* Predict the outcome of the experiment described in the attached protocol. (No protocol is attached.)

Level 1: Very low. The task requires a model of a single step. One familiar process advances once and nothing deflects it, so even a rough model lands on the same answer.
Examples:
* A wine glass is knocked off a table onto a tiled floor. What happens to it?
* A driver described as short-tempered is cut up at a junction. How do they react?
* A mug of coffee at 70 degrees is left in a room at 18 degrees. Is it warmer or cooler ten minutes later?

Level 2: Low. The task requires a model that a known rule already supplies. One process runs on, or several run on without acting on one another, and the rule yields the answer without stepping through the course. Critically, the task stays at this level however long the horizon and however exact the answer required, since neither makes the model more complex or more accurate.
Examples:
* A tap delivering six litres a minute runs into a 200-litre bath with the plug in. Does it overflow within the hour?
* Three kettles are switched on at once, each on its own hob and each holding a different amount of water. Have all three boiled within ten minutes?
* A line of forty dominoes is set up, each close enough to topple the next, and the first is pushed. Does the last one fall? (Each fall causes the next, but nothing feeds back, so a chain needs no coupled model.)
* A deposit of 4,200 earns 0.4 per cent compounded monthly with no withdrawals. In which month does the balance first exceed 5,000?
* A customer-service agent must move an order to a different address and swap one item for a cheaper variant. Stated rules settle what each change does, and the changes do not bear on one another.

Level 3: Intermediate. The task requires a model whose parts act on one another. A few processes influence each other in a way that changes the answer, so a model that leaves the coupling out gives the wrong result. The model itself may stay coarse, and once it captures the coupling, a loose run of it still lands on the answer.
Examples:
* One of three lanes closes on a busy ring road. Drivers divert onto the parallel streets, which fill and send some of them back. Do journey times across the district rise?
* Foxes reach a hillside of rabbits. More rabbits feed more foxes, and more foxes thin the rabbits. Do the rabbits keep increasing? (The same hillside appears at Level 4, asked more finely.)
* A cue ball strikes the pack slightly off-centre and the balls scatter. Does any ball at all reach a pocket? (The same break appears at Level 5.)
* A validation routine is reached by several call paths that share the same resolution machinery. A change is proposed to one of them. Do the others still behave?

Level 4: High. The task requires a model that is both coupled and accurate. The answer is fine enough that leaving out any one coupling flips it, and running the model loosely flips it too. Importantly, what places a task here is the accuracy the question forces rather than the number of parts the model has.
Examples:
* The same hillside of rabbits and foxes, cycling with a period of about four years. Does the rabbit population at the next trough fall below forty?
* A drought year on a smallholding whose crops share one irrigation channel, harbour pests that spread between them, and were planted on staggered dates. Which of the crops fail?
* Two queues at a single counter, where each arrival joins the shorter one and the server takes from them alternately. Given the arrival times, is the second queue ever empty while the first still holds three or more?

Level 5: Very high. The task requires a model that no one is known to have. The situation's indirect effects rival or outweigh its direct ones, because what the parts do to one another feeds back and does so again. Whether any model predicts such a situation accurately is itself unsettled, so a model built from the direct effects alone points the wrong way, and courses that begin alike can end far apart. Note, a task does not belong here because the law it turns on is obscure, which is a matter of knowledge. What places it here is that the second-order effects overturn the first and no established model is known to capture them.
Examples:
* The same billiards break. Which pocket, if any, does the ball that ends up nearest the cushion reach?
* From the described atmospheric conditions, which of two neighbouring valleys does the storm strike three days from now?
* A run begins on one of a town's two banks, each depositor's move shifting the odds for the next. Does it spread to the second bank?
* A novel pathogen is described three weeks into an outbreak, with the transmission and behaviour figures gathered so far. Does the wave peak before the vaccine arrives?
