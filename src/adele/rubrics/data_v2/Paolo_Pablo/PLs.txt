# Simulating

This rubric assesses the level of cognitive demands associated with simulating, which is the work of finding out how a situation will unfold. The situation is given, and what is asked is what happens next or what happens in the end. It may be physical, mechanical, biological, social or economic. What is simulated is what the world does, not what to do about it. The demand rises with how much change must be carried forward, and how finely. It falls where a model of the environment carries the change instead. A rule that covers the whole course does the carrying, whether that rule is a physical law, a dynamic stated in the task, or a familiar regularity. The answer is then computed rather than tracked. Where no rule covers the course, the detail has to be carried step by step.

What this dimension does not cover. Choosing among one's own actions is planning work, and a candidate action enters here only as a stipulated change whose consequences must be traced. Knowing the governing regularity is knowledge, and is not scored here. Finding out the current state is perception or search. Propagation is scored from the situation as given. Arithmetic is not simulating, so computing an exact value along an undeflected course does not raise this demand. Neither does a situation's inner churn, where the answer survives a rough account of it. Other agents enter as processes, and their described tendencies are propagated like any other. Working out what they believe or want does not raise this demand.

Level 0: None. Tasks at this level require no simulating. Nothing has to be run forward. The situation is static, its outcome is already stated, or the question is about the state as given. A situation named but not presented also belongs here, because with nothing given there is nothing to run.
Examples:
* Given the full record of a finished match, say which side won.
* Say whether a completed Sudoku grid is valid.
* Given the guest list and the seating rules, say whether the seating chart breaks any of them.

Level 1: Very low. Tasks at this level require the outcome to be read off a single step. One familiar process advances once, and nothing deflects it. The outcome follows at once, and a rough projection lands on it.
Examples:
* A glass is knocked off a table onto a stone floor. Say what happens to it.
* A match is touched to dry straw. Say what happens.
* A driver is described as short-tempered. Another car cuts in front of them. Say how they react.

Level 2: Low. Tasks at this level require a course to be carried forward under a rule that covers it. One process runs on, or several run on without touching each other. A known regularity covers the whole way, so the answer can be computed directly or projected roughly. Neither the length of the horizon nor the exactness of the answer raises the demand.
Examples:
* A tap runs into a bath with the plug in. Say whether it overflows before morning.
* Ice sits in a drink left in full sun. Say whether it has melted by evening.
* A ball is thrown at a given speed and angle. Ignoring air resistance, compute exactly where it lands.

Level 3: Intermediate. Tasks at this level require an interaction to be worked through. A few processes influence one another, and the interaction changes the answer. Projecting each alone gives the wrong result, so the coupling has to be carried together. The answer itself stays coarse. Once the effect of the coupling is caught, a loose account still lands on it.
Examples:
* A lane closes on a busy road. Drivers move to the side streets, which fill and send some of them back. Say whether journeys across the area get longer.
* Foxes arrive on a hillside of rabbits. More rabbits feed more foxes, and more foxes thin the rabbits. Say whether the rabbits keep increasing.
* A shop raises its prices. Customers drift to the rival across the square, whose queues lengthen. Say whether the shop takes more money over the month.

Level 4: High. Tasks at this level require interlocking change to be held faithfully. The answer is fine enough that dropping any one coupling flips it, and tracking one loosely flips it too. No single process settles the answer by its rough course. What places a task here is the fineness the answer demands, not the number of couplings.
Examples:
* A drought year on a smallholding. The crops share one irrigation channel, each harbours pests that reach the others, and the planting dates are staggered. Say which crops fail.
* A machine of tilting trays, rolling balls and counterweights is set going. Say whether the bell at the end rings.
* A predator is removed from a described food web. Say which populations rise and which crash.

Level 5: Very high. Tasks at this level require diverging courses to be told apart. Small differences early grow as the change plays out, so nearby starts end far apart. The task asks which of those courses the situation actually takes. No rule compresses the run, so the fineness of the level below has to be held the whole way. Divergence on its own does not place a task here. Where a coarse answer would survive it, the task sits where its own question puts it.
Examples:
* From a described break, say which pocket the ball nearest the cushion reaches.
* From described conditions, say which of two neighbouring valleys the storm strikes three days out.
* A run starts on one bank. Each depositor's move shifts the odds for the next, and the town sits between the two outcomes. Say whether the run spreads to the second bank.
