This rubric evaluates the simulating demands of tasks across six levels of difficulty, focusing exclusively on the anticipation of how a given situation unfolds, which is required whenever a task presents a state of affairs and asks what it does next rather than what to do about it. This includes situations that are physical, mechanical, biological, social or economic. Each level increases in complexity based on the amount of interacting change that must be carried forward and on how finely the answer distinguishes between the ways the situation could go. The demand stays low wherever a rule yields the answer without stepping through the course, whether that rule is a physical law, a dynamic stated in the task, or a familiar regularity. Where no such rule applies, the change must be carried step by step, and the demand rises with how much of it interacts and how little of it may be dropped.

What this dimension does not cover. Choosing among one's own actions is planning, and a candidate action enters here only as a stipulated change whose consequences must be traced. Knowing the governing regularity is a matter of knowledge rather than of simulating, so a task does not become more demanding here because the law it turns on is obscure. Finding out the current state is perception or search, and propagation is scored from the situation as given. Computing an exact value along a course that nothing deflects is arithmetic, and does not raise this demand. Neither does a situation's inner churn, wherever the answer survives a rough account of it. Other agents enter as processes whose described tendencies are propagated like any other, while working out what they believe or want belongs elsewhere.

Level 0: None. The task requires no simulating. Nothing has to be run forward, because the situation is static, its outcome is already stated, or the question concerns the state as it is given. Note, a situation that the task names but does not present belongs here too, since nothing is supplied to run.
Examples:
* The full move list of a finished chess game is given. Which side won?
* Here is a completed Sudoku grid. Is it valid?
* Predict the outcome of the experiment described in the attached protocol. (No protocol is attached.)

Level 1: Very low. The task requires the outcome to be read off a single step of change. A familiar process advances once and nothing deflects it, so any rough projection lands on the same answer.
Examples:
* A wine glass is knocked off a table onto a tiled floor. What happens to it?
* A driver described as short-tempered is cut up at a junction. How do they react?
* A mug of coffee at 70 degrees is left in a room at 18 degrees. Is it warmer or cooler ten minutes later?

Level 2: Low. The task requires a course to be projected under a rule that already covers it. One process runs on, or several run on without touching one another, and a known regularity yields the answer without stepping through the course. Critically, a task remains at this level however long the horizon and however exact the answer required, since neither of those adds anything to carry.
Examples:
* A tap delivering six litres a minute runs into a 200-litre bath with the plug in. Does it overflow within the hour?
* Twelve kettles are switched on at once, each on its own hob and each holding a different amount of water. Have all twelve boiled within ten minutes?
* A line of forty dominoes is set up, each close enough to topple the next, and the first is pushed. Does the last one fall? (Each fall causes the next, but nothing feeds back, so a chain is not a coupling.)
* A deposit of 4,200 earns 0.4 per cent compounded monthly with no withdrawals. In which month does the balance first exceed 5,000?

Level 3: Intermediate. The task requires an interaction to be worked through. A few processes influence one another in a way that changes the answer, so projecting each alone gives the wrong result and the coupling must be carried together. The answer itself remains coarse, and once the effect of the coupling is caught, a loose account still lands on it.
Examples:
* One of three lanes closes on a busy ring road. Drivers divert onto the parallel streets, which fill and send some of them back. Do journey times across the district rise?
* Foxes reach a hillside of rabbits. More rabbits feed more foxes, and more foxes thin the rabbits. Do the rabbits keep increasing? (The same hillside appears at Level 4, asked more finely.)
* A cue ball strikes the pack slightly off-centre and the balls scatter. Does any ball at all reach a pocket? (The same break appears at Level 5.)

Level 4: High. The task requires interlocking change to be held faithfully throughout. The answer is fine enough that dropping any one coupling flips it, and so does tracking one loosely, so no single process settles the matter by its rough course. Importantly, what places a task here is the fineness the answer demands rather than the number of couplings involved.
Examples:
* The same hillside, cycling with a period of about four years. Does the rabbit population at the next trough fall below forty?
* A drought year on a smallholding whose crops share one irrigation channel, harbour pests that spread between them, and were planted on staggered dates. Which of the crops fail?
* Two queues at a single counter, where each arrival joins the shorter one and the server takes from them alternately. Given the arrival times, is the second queue ever empty while the first still holds three or more?

Level 5: Very high. The task requires diverging courses to be told apart. Small differences early grow as the change plays out, so courses that begin close together end far apart, and the task asks which of them the situation actually takes. No rule compresses such a run, so the fineness of the level below must be sustained the whole way. Note, divergence alone does not place a task here, and where a coarse answer would survive it the task belongs where its own question puts it.
Examples:
* The same break. Which pocket, if any, does the ball that ends up nearest the cushion reach?
* From the described atmospheric conditions, which of two neighbouring valleys does the storm strike three days from now?
* A run begins on one of a town's two banks, each depositor's move shifting the odds for the next. Does it spread to the second bank?
