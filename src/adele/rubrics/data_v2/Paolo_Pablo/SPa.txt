# Auditory processing
#! ADeLe v2 (agentic) | version: v2-r70 | source: Paolo_Pablo | revised: 2026-08-26 | provenance: docs/rubric-provenance/SPa.md

This rubric assesses the difficulty level of tasks requiring information to be recovered from an auditory signal. What sets this difficulty apart is how much of what the task needs the sound itself carries, and how much must be supplied from beyond it. It ranges from tasks that carry no auditory signal, through sounds that present the content plainly, hold it under other sound, or carry only part of it, to sounds that do not carry the content at all and yield it only by reasoning back through how they were produced or propagated. Information is carried when it is physically present in the sound, whatever frequency range or temporal precision drawing it out would take. A sound carries more than is obvious from it. The direction it came from is written into how it differs across the points it reaches, the size and material of what made it into the ring and decay it leaves, and the shape of the space it crossed into the way it reverberates. Content of that kind is present, only hard to bring out, and sits at the level its recovery sets. A signal may reach several points at once. What is captured together, in fixed relation, is one signal, and what its parts jointly hold is carried by it, as the difference between two channels holds a direction. What the signal does not physically hold is not carried, and content of that kind reaches the top level only where it can be recovered by working back through how the sound was formed. This keeps the scale a property of the signal and the task rather than of the acuity brought to recovering it.

What this dimension does not cover. This dimension concerns getting the content out of the signal rather than what is done with it afterwards. What the recovered content means, what a speaker intends, feels or implies, does not raise this demand, however clear or unclear the audio was. Choosing which of several sources to attend to is search rather than extraction, and does not raise this demand, though separating the chosen source from the others does. How long listening must be sustained is not itself perceptual demand. Producing sound or music is generation rather than perception, and is not scored here. Note, acquiring an accent is not scored either, since it describes a solver rather than a task.

Level 0: None. The task carries no auditory signal, or any sound it carries is incidental and nothing the task needs is heard from it.
Examples:
* Answer a question about a passage of supplied text.
* Summarise a meeting supplied as a written transcript.
* Decide which of two written arguments is better supported.

Level 1: Very low. The task requires the information to be heard as presented. The sound carries it clearly, unmasked and at ordinary level, and nothing has to be recovered. Note, however long or fast the sound runs, the task stays here so long as what it needs is plainly audible, since length is not itself perceptual demand.
Examples:
* Transcribe a sentence spoken clearly by one speaker in a quiet room.
* State which of two clearly played tones is the higher.
* Identify what an object is made of from the clean sound of it being struck, where the material's ring is plainly present.

Level 2: Low. The task requires the information to be separated. The target sound is carried whole but overlaid with other sound, so it must be pulled out of the mixture before it can be heard. The source to follow is given rather than chosen. Critically, the whole of the target sound is still present under the mixture. Where part of it is missing, cut out, dropped or swallowed, the task is reconstruction and belongs at the level above.
Examples:
* Transcribe the named speaker at a dinner table where several people talk at once.
* Follow the cello line in a recording in which the other instruments play different material.
* Fix the direction a named sound came from in a stereo recording, where both channels carry it and the difference between them holds the answer.

Level 3: Intermediate. The task requires the information to be reconstructed. The sound carries only part of the content, the rest clipped, dropped or masked, so what survives must be completed from the regularities of the domain, from how speech, music or a mechanism usually sounds. Critically, however badly the signal is degraded, the task stays at this level so long as what survives carries enough to complete the content from within.
Examples:
* Transcribe a phone call in which bursts of dropout remove whole syllables that must be inferred from the words around them.
* Identify a word spoken through a closing door.
* Recover a sentence from a recording clipped so heavily that many consonants are lost.

Level 4: High. The task requires the information to be combined. No one signal carries the content, so several, arriving separately at different times, places or through different channels, with their relation to one another not given, must first be brought into correspondence, each alone leaving the content undetermined. What raises the demand is that the correspondence between the parts must itself be established before the content exists at all. Note, where the parts already stand in a fixed, known relation, they are one signal and the task belongs below.
Examples:
* Fix where a sound came from using recordings made independently at two separate points, whose timing must first be aligned before the position can emerge from the pair.
* Reconstruct one sentence from several partial recordings, each missing a different stretch of it.
* Establish what was said by combining a poor audio track with a video of the speaker's mouth, neither being sufficient alone.

Level 5: Very high. The task requires the information to be inverted. The sound does not carry the content as content at all, and recovery depends on modelling how it was produced or propagated and working back through that process, through the acoustics of the space, the mechanism of the source, or the encoding itself. The content cannot be heard in the signal, because it is not present there. Only the trace of the sound's formation is, and the content follows by inverting that formation.
Examples:
* Infer the size and shape of a room from the reverberation left on a single passive recording made inside it.
* Determine the length and tension of an unseen string from the timbre of the single note it sounds.
* Recover the shape of a sealed cavity from the resonances it produces when air passes across its mouth.
